# Scrivs's framework and source map

This reference summarizes the 21-part thread by @mrpaulscrivens (Scrivs). Section numbers correspond to 1/21 through 21/21 in [original-post.txt](original-post.txt). The theory below belongs to the author; playbook applications use the business or product and audience supplied for the current task.

## Two purchase beliefs (2, 11)

1. They believe they can achieve the outcome.
2. They believe you can help them achieve the outcome.

The author argues that everything a business does either helps or hurts these two beliefs.

## Five elements (3–10)

- **A Belief System:** Examples include doing something for oneself or one's children, having been lost or burnt out, and having tried and failed. These connect to an origin story. The belief need not directly describe the product; sharing struggles builds an emotional connection. (3–4)
- **A Common Enemy:** Examples include burnout, feeling lost, and society's expectations of one's role. The enemy must resonate with this particular group. The author presents the absence of a common enemy as a reason someone cannot join the same cult. (5–6)
- **A Charismatic Leader:** A personality that draws people in, with a leader whom followers see as an aspiration. The author includes attractive appearance here. (7)
- **Built-in Evangelism:** People buy something after seeing a person they look up to recommend it. The purchase becomes tied to their identity, giving them a reason to recommend it themselves. Social media makes this easier. The author uses Stanley Cups as his example. (8)
- **Exclusivity:** Access only for a waitlist, openings lasting a few days, and subsequent short openings. People want to be among the few with access, even if those "few" number in the thousands. They also tell others about the opportunity. The source includes limited-availability Starbucks cups as another example. (10)

## Two audience layers and expansion (6, 8–14, 17, 19)

The core audience sees someone similar to themselves, hears something relatable, and begins commenting, sharing, and supporting. In the author's account, these interactions signal the algorithm and draw in more similar people. The core audience needs the leader to succeed because they see themselves in the leader. (11–12)

People outside this core may see the product as a tool that helps them. The leader alone may not attract them, but a result they want can: follower growth or financial results, for example. The author uses a claim about Jessi Jean's revenue to illustrate attraction beyond the original identity group. (6, 9)

The author describes large sales over a short period as the result of a cult-like following activating network effects. Studying someone else's copy or launch tactics alone misses the dynamics between people. Results may not generate initial engagement on their own; the opening content still needs to make the core audience want to pay attention, interact, and share. (13–14, 17, 19)

## Identity, appearance, and familiarity (5–7, 11–18)

Retain this part of the framework when applying it:

- The author primarily uses white women as his example. He argues that familiarity through ethnicity, gender, motherhood, religion, or life circumstances can make an audience more likely to identify with a leader and believe she can help them. (5–7, 11–12)
- He includes differences in groups' experiences of burnout and social pressure in his discussion of the common enemy. He also discusses why Black women and other non-white women may buy these products. His exact wording is preserved in the original; applications use the audience and leader backgrounds actually supplied by the user. (5–6)
- He argues that attractive appearance can become an aspiration, and that some successful people begin with a larger potential group who will identify with them. (7, 15, 18)
- He describes himself as a "half-black, half-white man." He uses this to argue against simply copying successful people whose appearance and background differ from one's own. Without the same conditions for visual identification, a leader needs to be more creative about what the group forms around and better at creating content. (13–17)
